First, what we actually do. We scan buildings and places using an algorithm called 3D Gaussian Splatting, and turn them into spaces you can walk through in a web browser.

Why that matters here: in Korea, places disappear constantly. A graduation exhibition lasts only five days. A neighbourhood is redeveloped. And the digital twin is usually offered as the way to keep them.

But keep what, exactly? The space? What happened in it? Or the community itself — can a digital twin hold that? That is the question both of these projects were really about.

Very briefly, how the technology works — because one property of it drives everything that follows.

Alberti gave European painting a space constructed from a single fixed point. Cézanne stopped obeying that, and built his mountain out of many separate glances held on one canvas. 3DGS does something close to the second: it optimises a space out of thousands of them.

Concretely: you walk through a space with a camera. Structure-from-motion estimates where each photograph was taken. The system then fits a cloud of translucent ellipsoids — Gaussians — each with a position, a shape, an opacity, and a colour that changes with viewing direction. It renders that cloud from a known camera, compares it to the real photograph, and adjusts. Tens of thousands of times.

Note what is absent. No mesh. No surfaces. No geometry. It reconstructs how a place looks, not what it is made of.

[45초. 유일한 기술 설명. 빠르게 지나가되 마지막 문장은 또렷하게.]

A playground we scanned. Everything is sharp except the person walking through it.

The optimization assumes the scene did not change while you were photographing it.

  • Anything that moved is not reconstructed as an object — it is baked in as a smear.
  • What survives is that he was there. What is lost is where he was.
  • So the medium is good at space on the condition that events are excluded.

All of it rests on one assumption: that the scene did not change while you were photographing it.

Here is a playground we scanned. Everything is sharp — the slide, the railing, the paving. Except this. That is a person who walked while we were filming. He broke the assumption, so he was not reconstructed as an object; he was baked into the scene as a smear. What survives is that he was there. What is lost is where he was.

So this medium is good at space on the condition that events are excluded. And a community is not geometry — geometry is only the substrate it sits on. What makes it a community is a second layer laid over the physical one: the events. People meeting, someone presenting, someone stopping in front of a drawing for five minutes.

Which gave us a straightforward thing to test: can we add that layer back on top? That is what the first project was.

If the two spaces act on each other they become one event; if they become one event, the online and offline participants become one community. That was the bet.

To place it against the two things it usually gets confused with. A metaverse is an alternative space — parallel to the real one, not touching it. A digital twin is a mirror space — the real world writes into the virtual one, one direction only.

What we wanted was the third: causal coupling, both directions. The reasoning was that if the two spaces act on each other they become one event, and if they become one event, the people online and the people in the room become one community.

That was the bet.

Three findings from this.

One. Presence before synchronization. We spent our design effort on the coupling; what carried the project was the fidelity of simply being there.

Two. We measured the wrong thing. We built metrics for interaction — the metric that mattered was how long the space stayed open. And here is the sharp version: I can tell you exactly how many people visited the virtual exhibition. Eight hundred and thirteen. I cannot tell you how many visited the physical one. We never counted. That asymmetry is what a digital twin actually gives you — it makes presence countable. It is also the trap, because it is precisely what was countable that we optimised for.

Three. Not simultaneity, but asynchrony. Nobody wanted to be in two places at once; they wanted to be in one place, later. What joined the online and offline participants was not shared time. It was a shared place that stayed open.
