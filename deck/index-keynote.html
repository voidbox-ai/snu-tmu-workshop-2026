Rethinking Digital Twins for Community Rebuilding

Hyeogjin Noh  ·  CEO, voidbox
SNU × TMU Annual Workshop

Good morning. My name is Hyeogjin Noh, and I run a company called voidbox. We build 3D scanning and spatial software for architecture.

[이름과 회사만. 논지 미리 말하지 않기. 10초.]

Places disappear faster than
communities can remember them.

A graduation exhibition lasts five days. A school gets remodeled. A neighbourhood is redeveloped. In Korea this is not an occasional event — it is the normal condition.

The digital twin is offered as the answer to this. Keep the place. But keep what about it? The space? Or what happened in it?

Today I want to report on one attempt to answer that question. And I should say at the start — most of what I have to report is failure.

[마지막 문장에서 속도를 늦출 것. 이 발표의 계약을 여기서 맺는다.]

Cézanne painted this mountain more than thirty times. And inside a single canvas he stopped obeying the single vanishing point that Alberti had given European painting four centuries earlier. He built the mountain out of many separate glances, held together on one surface.

That is almost exactly what our scanning does. So let me show you how it actually works.

[25초. 여기서 그림 감상하지 말 것 — 원리 설명으로 넘어가는 다리일 뿐.]

How 3D Gaussian Splatting works

You walk through a space holding a camera, and the space is decomposed into thousands of images. Structure-from-motion estimates where each of those photographs was taken from.

From there the system estimates a cloud of translucent ellipsoids — Gaussians. Each one carries a position, a shape, an opacity, and a colour that changes depending on the direction you look at it from.

Then the loop. Render the cloud from a camera position the system knows. Compare that render to the actual photograph taken from that position. Nudge every ellipsoid to reduce the difference. Repeat, tens of thousands of times.

[루프를 손으로 한 번 그리며 설명. 40초.]

No mesh. No surfaces. No geometry.

3DGS does not reconstruct what a place is made of.
It reconstructs how a place looks.

This is the important difference from photogrammetry, or from LiDAR. Those techniques chase surfaces — they try to recover the geometry of the thing. This does not. There is no model in here. There is only appearance.

Hold onto that. It comes back at the end of this talk, twice.

[30초. "twice"에서 살짝 멈춤 — 청중이 기억하게.]

And it rests on one assumption.

The optimization assumes the scene
did not change while you were
photographing it.

And all of it rests on a single assumption. The optimization assumes the scene did not change while you were photographing it.

[한 번만 말하고 바로 다음 슬라이드. 설명 덧붙이지 말 것.]

What happens when something moves

What survives is that he was there.
What is lost is where he was.

This is a playground we scanned. Everything in it is sharp. The slide, the railing, the paving stones.

Except this. This is a person. He walked while we were photographing. He violated the assumption — so he was not reconstructed as an object. He was baked into the scene as a smear.

What survives is that he was there. What is lost is where he was.

And I want to state the consequence as precisely as I can. 3DGS is not merely weak at events. It is good at space on the condition that events are excluded.

Now — a community is not geometry. Geometry is only the substrate a community sits on. What makes it a community is a second layer, laid over the physical one: the events. People meeting. Someone presenting. Someone stopping in front of a drawing for five minutes.

So if a digital twin is going to hold a community, a layer of events has to be added on top of the geometry. Our scan reconstructs the substrate almost perfectly. On its own it cannot carry the layer.

So we decided to build that second layer ourselves. That was our hypothesis, and we built an experiment to test it.

[이 발표에서 가장 중요한 60초. 서두르지 말 것. "on the condition that events are excluded"가 핵심 문장.]

The experiment

Physical · June 4–8, 2026

Virtual · opened June 5, 2026 — still open

Yonsei University, Dept. of Architectural Engineering
Graduation Exhibition 2026  ·  voidbox × Yonsei, MOU

Earlier this year we signed an agreement with the Department of Architectural Engineering at Yonsei University, to run their graduation exhibition in two places at once.

The physical exhibition ran for five days, from the fourth to the eighth of June. We scanned the hall, and the virtual exhibition opened on the fifth of June — one day later.

It is still open. I will come back to that.

["It is still open"에 살짝 무게. 나중에 회수한다.]

What we built

  • A 3DGS scan of the exhibition hall
  • A curation tool for placing works inside the scanned space
  • An archive site — navigation, notices, works
  • An interaction layer — comments, and two-way sync between the physical and the virtual exhibition

Four things. We scanned the hall. We built a curation tool so the department could place each student's work into the scanned space. We built the archive site around it.

And we built an interaction layer — comments, and a two-way sync between what was happening in the physical exhibition and what was happening in the virtual one.

That last one is the second layer — our attempt to lay events over the geometry. It is what this talk is about, and it is where most of our design effort went.

[네 번째 항목에서 손으로 가리키며 강조.]

The hypothesis

If the two spaces act on each other, they become one event.
If they become one event, online and offline participants become one community.

Let me put it against the two things it is usually confused with. A metaverse is an alternative space — it runs in parallel to the real one and does not touch it. A digital twin is a mirror space — the real world writes into the virtual one, in one direction.

What we wanted was the third thing. Causal coupling. Both directions. Something happens in the hall and the virtual exhibition changes; something happens in the virtual exhibition and the hall changes.

Because our reasoning was: if the two spaces act on each other, they become one event. And if they become one event, the people online and the people in the room become one community.

That was the bet.

["That was the bet." 짧게 끊고 바로 넘길 것.]

Eleven interactions in total. Across five days, one department, an entire graduating class.

And look at the asymmetry, because that is the part that taught me something. Two events crossed from the physical side into the virtual one. Zero crossed the other way. The coupling we designed was bidirectional — and in practice, one of the two directions never ran at all. The virtual exhibition was never able to act on the physical one. It could only watch.

But at some point I stopped asking why people didn't use what we had built, and started asking what they did instead.

How long each exhibition lived

The physical exhibition was dismantled in June. The virtual one is open right now, while I am standing here.

Not because of the interaction we designed.

And it was not because of the interaction layer. That, as we established, was used eleven times.

The feedback came back, again and again, in some version of the same sentence: this is close enough that I don't feel I missed it.

That is the one thing we did not design for. And it is the only thing that worked.

[마지막 문장, 담담하게. 자조하지 말 것.]

Three lessons

01

Presence before synchronization.

We optimized the coupling. What carried the project was the fidelity of simply being there.

02

We measured the wrong thing.

We built metrics for interaction. The metric that mattered was how long the space stayed open.

03

Not simultaneity, but asynchrony.

Nobody wanted to be in two places at once. They wanted to be in one place, later.

One. Presence before synchronization. We spent our design effort on the coupling. What actually carried the project was the fidelity of simply being there.

Two. We measured the wrong thing. And here is the sharpest version of that. I can tell you exactly how many people visited the virtual exhibition — eight hundred and thirteen. I cannot tell you how many visited the physical one. We never counted.

That asymmetry is what a digital twin actually gives you. It makes presence countable. And it is also the trap, because countable is not the same as meaningful — it is precisely because interaction was countable that we optimized for it.

Three. Not simultaneity, but asynchrony. Nobody wanted to be in two places at once. They wanted to be in one place, later. What joined the online and the offline participants was not shared time. It was a shared place that stayed open.

[1분에 세 개. 2번 lesson의 "we never counted" 부분만 조금 천천히, 나머지는 빠르게.]

If what we actually deliver is presence —
who else needs it?

So we asked a different question. If what we actually deliver is presence, who else needs it?

Our answer, for now, is not an audience.

The twin as an instrument, not an exhibit

  • Existing-condition survey. The scan stands in for measuring a building whose drawings are old or incomplete.
  • Design review. Our proposal is overlaid onto the scan and judged inside the existing place — not against an abstracted model.

The user of the twin here is not a visitor.
It is the designer.

Two uses. First, existing-condition survey. This is a rural school built decades ago; the drawings are old and incomplete. The scan stands in for going out and measuring it.

Second — and this is the one I find more interesting — design review. We overlay our proposal directly onto the scan. Which means the design gets judged inside the existing place, rather than against an abstracted model of it.

The user of the twin here is not a visitor. It is the designer. Same capability, completely different consumer.

[설계안 오버레이 화면을 손으로 가리키며. 45초.]

The scan will outlive
the school's current state.

Gaussians are appearance, not geometry — so alongside them we extract a low-density point cloud. Not survey-grade, but dimensionable.
Competition result pending.

And notice that the same thing is happening again. The scan will outlive the school's current state. At Yonsei that was an accident. Here it is deliberate. This building is about to be rebuilt for its community, and the scan is the last full record of what it was before.

One technical note, since I said earlier that Gaussians are appearance and not geometry. That is true — but alongside them we extract a low-density point cloud. It is not survey-grade, and I would not hand it to a structural engineer. But it is dimensionable, which is what makes this usable in an actual design process.

The competition result is not out yet.

Backup · Husserl

Abschattung — "adumbration"

"A thing in space is given to us, essentially,
only through adumbrations."

— Edmund Husserl, Ideen I (1913), §41

The object is never given whole. It is given as a series of partial profiles, which consciousness synthesises across the movement of the body.
3DGS is arguably that idea turned into an algorithm.

Only if asked about the theoretical background. Husserl: perception of a spatial object is always partial — you get profiles, adumbrations, and you synthesise them across your own bodily movement. Map it onto the pipeline: the object is the scanned space, the adumbrations are the input images, the bodily movement is the scanner's walking path, the synthesis is the optimization.

[Q&A 전용. 먼저 꺼내지 말 것.]

Backup · Borges

"…the Cartographers Guild drew a Map of the Empire whose size was that of the Empire, coinciding point for point with it. Succeeding Generations… delivered it up to the Inclemencies of Sun and Winters."

— Jorge Luis Borges, "Del rigor en la ciencia", 1946

The 1:1 map was completed — and then abandoned.

If asked whether we are building Borges' 1:1 map. The honest answer from our data: fidelity alone did not make the archive valuable, and it did not make it useless either. What made it valuable was that it stayed open. Borges' map failed because nobody could stand inside it.

[Q&A 전용.]

Backup · Archive and repertoire

Archive

Knowledge preserved in enduring media — text, image, object, building.

Repertoire

Knowledge transmitted only through embodied practice — gesture, speech, ritual, encounter.

We are very good at the archive.
The repertoire is what we lost.

Diana Taylor, The Archive and the Repertoire, Duke University Press, 2003.

If asked what exactly was lost. Diana Taylor's distinction is the cleanest frame: 3DGS is by nature an archive medium, and the events at an exhibition are repertoire. Our nine comments were an attempt to graft repertoire onto archive, and it did not take.
